# English Grammar / Subject and Predicate / Finding the Subject of a Sentence

# Finding the Subject of a Sentence

# Introduction

This chapter provides a clear, structured guide to identifying the **subject of a sentence**. It covers:

- The meaning and role of the subject
- A step-by-step method to find the subject
- Simple and complete subjects
- Subjects in different sentence types
- Compound subjects and subject complements
- Subjects as words, phrases, and clauses
- Dummy subjects (*it* and *there is*)

## 1. What Is the Subject?

**Definition:**

The **subject** of a sentence is the word or group of words that **tells who or what performs the action** or **who or what the sentence is about**.

### Basic Examples

- **The sun** rises in the east.
- **Rahim** is reading a book.
- **Honesty** is the best policy.
- **Bangladesh** is the greatest country. [***my personal opinion***]

In each sentence, the subject answers one simple question:

> **Who or what + verb?**

---

## 2. How to Find the Subject (Step-by-Step Method)

### Step 1: Find the Verb

The verb shows action or state of being.

Example: The little boy **is playing** in the yard.

Verb: *is playing*

### Step 2: Ask a Question

Ask: **Who is playing?**

Answer: *the little boy*

So, **the little boy** is the subject.

---

## 3. Simple Subject and Complete Subject

### 3.1 Simple Subject

The **simple subject** is the **main noun or pronoun** without modifiers.

Example: The tall, intelligent girl won the prize.

- Simple subject: **girl**

### 3.2 Complete Subject

The **complete subject** includes the noun **and all words describing it**.

Example: The tall, intelligent girl won the prize.

- Complete subject: **The tall, intelligent girl**

---

## 4. Subject in Different Sentence Types

### 4.1 Declarative Sentences (Statements)

These sentences state facts or opinions.

Example: **Water** boils at 100°C.

Subject: *Water*

---

### 4.2 Interrogative Sentences (Questions)

In questions, the subject often comes **after the verb or auxiliary verb**.

Example: **Are** you ready?

Rearranged: You **are** ready.

Subject: **you**

---

### 4.3 Imperative Sentences (Commands)

Imperative sentences usually have a **hidden subject**.

Example: Close the door.

Hidden subject: **You**

(You) close the door.

(you) edit the wiki.

---

### 4.4 Exclamatory Sentences

These express strong emotion.

Example: **What a beautiful day** it is!

Subject: **it**

---

## 5. Compound Subjects

A **compound subject** has **two or more subjects** joined by conjunctions such as *and, or*.

Examples:

- **Rahim and Karim** are best friends.
- **Books and knowledge** are closely related.

Each noun/pronoun is part of the subject.

---

## 6. Subject Complements (Not Subjects!)

Sometimes learners confuse subjects with words that **follow linking verbs**.

Example:

Rahim is **a doctor**.

- Subject: **Rahim**
- Subject complement: *a doctor*

Rule: The subject usually comes **before** the verb in statements.

---

## 7. Subjects as Different Parts of Speech

### 7.1 Noun as Subject

### **Honesty** is important.

### 7.2 Pronoun as Subject

### **She** is my sister.

### 7.3 Gerand as Subject (Verb + -ing)

### **Reading** improves knowledge.

### 7.4 Infinitive as Subject

### **To err** (to make a mistake) is human. {proverb}

### 7.5 Clause as Subject

### **What he said** surprised everyone.

---

## 8. Dummy (Empty) Subjeccts

Sometimes English uses a **dummy subject** when the real subject is not important or comes later.

### 8.1 “It” as Dummy Subject

### **It** is raining. **It** is important to study regularly.

### 8.2 “There” as Dummy Subject

### **There** is a problem. **There** are many students in the class.
